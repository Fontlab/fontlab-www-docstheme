    Product pages¶

    A product page connects a clear task to visible evidence and a next step. Lead with the work the reader wants to do, then show the interface or output that supports the claim. Keep compatibility, pricing and licensing in their current authoritative destinations.

    The examples use the shared theme and setup. Keep the .fltheme-components wrapper around Basecoat and du- component markup.

    Show the work beside the claim¶

    Once you have named the task, show what the reader will inspect. A screenshot needs a caption that explains its role. Keep the product description as text so it remains readable when the image cannot load.

    Put the demonstration beside the opening claim, not at the foot of the page, and let the first screen answer two questions: what is it, and what does it do for me? A text block near the top carries the phrase people search for. Use only fonts we own, fonts whose licence allows modified publication, or fonts with written permission in screenshots and demonstrations.

    This feature adapts the Vexy Lines introduction. The picture shows the actual workspace; the text explains the relationship between source, fill and layer.

    Direct the lines¶

    Vexy Lines transforms photos and images into vector artwork. A fill interprets the source's tone and structure as lines, dots or other marks. You can combine fills with layers, groups and masks to give different parts of the image different treatments.

    Start with one source and one fill. Inspect the result at the size where you intend to use it, then add another treatment where the composition needs one.

    Explain the handoff¶

    The FontLab conversion source describes the information that should survive conversion. The blog's TransType article explains why that information matters: a font contains decisions made during its design, not just a collection of outlines. This three-part composition gives those decisions room.

    Keep the source¶

    Retain the original files. Names, family structure, outlines, spacing and kerning give you something concrete to compare after conversion.

    Convert the family¶

    TransType is FontLab's dedicated tool for converting and organising font families. Check the current documentation for the formats and workflow you need before you begin.

    TransType product information

    Inspect the result¶

    Open the converted fonts in the application that will use them. Compare the names and family grouping, then inspect the outlines, spacing and kerning. Further design work belongs in a font editor.

    Answer the remaining decision¶

    Put purchase conditions at the destination that maintains them. Use a disclosure for supporting explanation, but keep the primary action visible. If the page shows ratings or reviews, show them as received, not filtered by stars, with a link to the full set.

    Find the right route for your files¶

    Conversion preserves an existing design in a usable format. Editing changes the design or its behaviour. If you need both, check each stage of the workflow against the source files and the destination application.

    What should I check before choosing?

    Identify the source formats, the target applications and the information that must survive. Check the product's current compatibility and licence terms. Keep an original copy of the files for comparison.

    End with the whole offer¶

    Before the last action, gather what the reader collected along the page: what they receive, who qualifies, the price and currency, the requirements that would otherwise send them looking (system, file formats, licence terms) and the exit terms. Give the page one offer for its reader, and name the action on the button. On a long page, show the terms near the top and again before the final button.

    Captions under product images are read more often than the body text. Write each one as a complete claim with the product name, so it still works for the reader who looks only at the pictures.

    Three small blocks help near the end. A “what you receive” list names the files, licence and access the buyer gets. A licence row states whether the licence expires and what the next major version is expected to cost, or that the cost is not yet set. A “reviewed on” date under the facts tells readers and answer engines that someone checked them.

    Pages for particular readers¶

    Some readers need a page of their own rather than a paragraph:

    • A customer project. One named person's real work: the brief, the files, what they did in the product, what was hard, the result, with their permission and the date.
    • The reader who must ask someone else. A page a designer can forward to whoever approves software spending: price per seat, licence terms, invoices, what the team gets, in plain language.
    • A paid landing page. One action and a minimal menu, the same facts and terms as the product page, and kept out of search results if it exists only for paid traffic.
    • A free guide. The guide itself, readable without a form; any form offers something extra, such as test files or update notices.
